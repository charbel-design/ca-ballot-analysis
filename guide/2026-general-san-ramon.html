Proposition 1

No

Medium

Why no. The measure would authorize $11.25 billion in general-obligation bonds, $10 billion for affordable rental and homeownership programs and $1.25 billion for veterans’ home, farm, and mobilehome loans. A new long-term debt repaid by taxpayers fails this framework’s high bar for new obligations. The housing purpose is a real point the other way, which is why this is Medium rather than High.

Measure test · not these weights

Legislative statute. Official summaries used here are paraphrases of the Secretary of State’s June 29, 2026 numbering release. A local guide’s “about 40,000 homes” figure is not treated as the legislative analyst’s estimate.

Proposition 2

Yes

Medium

Why yes. The amendment raises the Budget Stabilization Account cap from 10 percent of general-fund tax proceeds to 20 percent, changes the capital-gains set-aside, and lets a May revision function as a budget-emergency proclamation when the constitutional conditions are met. A larger reserve matches the fiscal-discipline test and is not a new tax. The easier path to declaring a budget emergency is the point the other way.

Measure test · not these weights

Legislative constitutional amendment. The qualified-list title is an increase in the state’s rainy-day fund. This pick follows the amendment text in the numbering release, not a shorthand that the state may save “10 percent more.”

Proposition 5

Yes

Low

Why yes. It would drop the successor election when a statewide officer is recalled and fill the office under ordinary vacancy rules. That is a process change. It does not clearly hit the tax test or the liberty test. The lean is Yes because it ends a plurality side-election. I am not confident here.

Proposition 39

Medium

Why yes. It would require government-issued identification at the polls, or the last four digits of such an identification number on a mail ballot, and require the state to provide identification cards on request. One identification rule for every voter matches this framework’s rule-equality test. The cost, and the risk that a lawful voter is turned away, are the point the other way.

Proposition 40

No

Medium

Why no. It would impose a one-time tax of up to 5 percent on covered assets above $1 billion, excluding real property and some retirement accounts, and exempt that revenue from the state spending limit. A new tax plus a spending-limit opt-out fails the fiscal tests. San Ramon Patch describes the initiative as labor-union-sponsored. It stays Medium because the tax is one time and the payers are a narrow class.

Measure test · not these weights

Initiative constitutional amendment and statute. The analyst’s estimate says the tax would raise tens of billions over several years and that income-tax revenue would likely fall afterward. Ninety percent of the revenue is for health care and 10 percent for food assistance or education.

Measure RTM

No

Medium

Why no. It would add a retail sales tax of one-half of one percent in Contra Costa for 14 years, about $980 million a year, to avoid major transit cuts and to fund safety, repairs, and oversight. This framework disfavors new sales taxes, and the ballot question reads as revenue to cover an operating gap. Riders and the named repairs are a real benefit the other way, so this is Medium rather than High.

Measure test · not these weights

The county counsel’s analysis says the district was created by Senate Bill 63 (2025) and covers Alameda, Contra Costa, San Mateo, Santa Clara, and San Francisco. The San Francisco rate in that analysis is one percent. This pass did not review the yes and no campaign treasuries.
