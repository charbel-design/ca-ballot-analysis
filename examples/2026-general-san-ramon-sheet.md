## Measures

Measures are not scored on the seven weights.

### Proposition 1 — No, Medium

**Why no.** The measure would authorize $11.25 billion in general-obligation bonds, $10 billion for affordable rental and homeownership programs and $1.25 billion for veterans’ home, farm, and mobilehome loans. A new long-term debt repaid by taxpayers fails this framework’s high bar for new obligations. The housing purpose is a real point the other way, which is why this is Medium rather than High.

Legislative statute. Official summaries used here are paraphrases of the Secretary of State’s June 29, 2026 numbering release. A local guide’s “about 40,000 homes” figure is not treated as the legislative analyst’s estimate.

- [Numbering release](https://www.sos.ca.gov/administration/news-releases-and-advisories/2026-news-releases-and-advisories/secretary-state-shirley-n-weber-phd-assigns-numbers-november-ballot-measures-invites-ballot-arguments)
- [Qualified measures](https://www.sos.ca.gov/elections/ballot-measures/qualified-ballot-measures)

### Proposition 2 — Yes, Medium

**Why yes.** The amendment raises the Budget Stabilization Account cap from 10 percent of general-fund tax proceeds to 20 percent, changes the capital-gains set-aside, and lets a May revision function as a budget-emergency proclamation when the constitutional conditions are met. A larger reserve matches the fiscal-discipline test and is not a new tax. The easier path to declaring a budget emergency is the point the other way.

Legislative constitutional amendment. The qualified-list title is an increase in the state’s rainy-day fund. This pick follows the amendment text in the numbering release, not a shorthand that the state may save “10 percent more.”

- [Numbering release](https://www.sos.ca.gov/administration/news-releases-and-advisories/2026-news-releases-and-advisories/secretary-state-shirley-n-weber-phd-assigns-numbers-november-ballot-measures-invites-ballot-arguments)
- [Qualified measures](https://www.sos.ca.gov/elections/ballot-measures/qualified-ballot-measures)

### Proposition 3 — No, Medium

**Why no.** It would make the temporary high-income tax rates, now set to expire in 2031, permanent. The analyst’s summary says that keeps about $5 billion to $15 billion a year. A permanent tax fails the high bar. The payers are high-income filers, and the stated split is mostly schools, so this is Medium rather than High.

Initiative constitutional amendment. Thresholds in the summary are about $360,000 for single filers, $721,000 for joint filers, and $490,000 for heads of household, in 2024 dollars.

- [Numbering release](https://www.sos.ca.gov/administration/news-releases-and-advisories/2026-news-releases-and-advisories/secretary-state-shirley-n-weber-phd-assigns-numbers-november-ballot-measures-invites-ballot-arguments)

### Proposition 4 — No, Medium

**Why no.** It would repeal the ban on spending public money on campaigns for elective office, with exceptions and limits. Taxpayers would fund candidates they oppose. The argument that public funding reduces donor dependence is why this is Medium rather than High.

Legislative statute. This pass did not review the campaign’s funders.

- [Numbering release](https://www.sos.ca.gov/administration/news-releases-and-advisories/2026-news-releases-and-advisories/secretary-state-shirley-n-weber-phd-assigns-numbers-november-ballot-measures-invites-ballot-arguments)

### Proposition 5 — Yes, Low

**Why yes.** It would drop the successor election when a statewide officer is recalled and fill the office under ordinary vacancy rules. That is a process change. It does not clearly hit the tax test or the liberty test. The lean is Yes because it ends a plurality side-election. I am not confident here.

Legislative constitutional amendment. If the governor is recalled, the lieutenant governor serves the rest of the term, with a special election in the first two years if the recall falls before the next nomination period closes. The recalled officer cannot be appointed to the vacancy.

**What would settle it.** Whether you want the successor chosen on the same ballot.

### Proposition 37 — Yes, Low

**Why yes.** It authorizes up to $25 billion in bonds for down-payment loans of up to 17 percent of the price, for buyers under 200 percent of area median income who put at least 3 percent down. The official summary says borrowers repay the bonds and the analyst estimates no direct state or local cost. That is why this is not treated like Proposition 1. The structure is novel, so the confidence is Low.

Initiative statute. Push back if the repayment promise depends on a taxpayer backstop the summary does not show.

**What would settle it.** Whether repayment depends on a taxpayer backstop the official summary does not show.

- [Numbering release](https://www.sos.ca.gov/administration/news-releases-and-advisories/2026-news-releases-and-advisories/secretary-state-shirley-n-weber-phd-assigns-numbers-november-ballot-measures-invites-ballot-arguments)

### Proposition 38 — No, Medium

**Why no.** It would authorize $8.4 billion in general-obligation bonds for immunology research, about $500 million a year for 25 years from the general fund. New long-term debt on diffuse taxpayers fails the high bar. The disease-research purpose and the requirement to sell resulting drugs in California at 20 percent below the national average are the point the other way.

Initiative statute. Half the money goes to a University of California-affiliated institute and half to grants.

- [Qualified measures](https://www.sos.ca.gov/elections/ballot-measures/qualified-ballot-measures)

### Proposition 39 — Yes, Medium

**Why yes.** It would require government-issued identification at the polls, or the last four digits of such an identification number on a mail ballot, and require the state to provide identification cards on request. One identification rule for every voter matches this framework’s rule-equality test. The cost, and the risk that a lawful voter is turned away, are the point the other way.

Initiative constitutional amendment. The analyst’s estimate is tens of millions of dollars one time, and ongoing costs from the tens of millions to the low hundreds of millions. This pass did not review evidence about how often a lawful voter is turned away.

- [Numbering release](https://www.sos.ca.gov/administration/news-releases-and-advisories/2026-news-releases-and-advisories/secretary-state-shirley-n-weber-phd-assigns-numbers-november-ballot-measures-invites-ballot-arguments)
- [Qualified measures](https://www.sos.ca.gov/elections/ballot-measures/qualified-ballot-measures)

### Proposition 40 — No, Medium

**Why no.** It would impose a one-time tax of up to 5 percent on covered assets above $1 billion, excluding real property and some retirement accounts, and exempt that revenue from the state spending limit. A new tax plus a spending-limit opt-out fails the fiscal tests. San Ramon Patch describes the initiative as labor-union-sponsored. It stays Medium because the tax is one time and the payers are a narrow class.

Initiative constitutional amendment and statute. The analyst’s estimate says the tax would raise tens of billions over several years and that income-tax revenue would likely fall afterward. Ninety percent of the revenue is for health care and 10 percent for food assistance or education.

- [Numbering release](https://www.sos.ca.gov/administration/news-releases-and-advisories/2026-news-releases-and-advisories/secretary-state-shirley-n-weber-phd-assigns-numbers-november-ballot-measures-invites-ballot-arguments)

### Proposition 41 — Yes, High

**Why yes.** It would require audits of programs funded by new statewide special taxes and block new state taxes that opt out of the spending limit, including taxes on the same ballot. It does not create a tax, it blocks those opt-outs, and it requires the audits. No test of similar weight points the other way.

Initiative constitutional amendment. The analyst says the net fiscal effect is unknown. That is uncertainty about magnitude, not a test pointing the other way.

- [Numbering release](https://www.sos.ca.gov/administration/news-releases-and-advisories/2026-news-releases-and-advisories/secretary-state-shirley-n-weber-phd-assigns-numbers-november-ballot-measures-invites-ballot-arguments)

### Proposition 42 — Yes, High

**Why yes.** It would prohibit a new state tax on personal property, including retirement accounts, financial assets, business interests, and intellectual property, and prohibit certain retroactive state taxes, including measures on the same ballot. The lower future revenue the analyst flags is the intended effect under this framework. Three tests align and none points the other way.

Initiative constitutional amendment. The three tests are: no new tax of this kind, no retroactive tax of the kind described, and the rule covers same-ballot measures.

- [Numbering release](https://www.sos.ca.gov/administration/news-releases-and-advisories/2026-news-releases-and-advisories/secretary-state-shirley-n-weber-phd-assigns-numbers-november-ballot-measures-invites-ballot-arguments)
- [Qualified measures](https://www.sos.ca.gov/elections/ballot-measures/qualified-ballot-measures)

### Proposition 43 — Yes, Medium

**Why yes.** Beginning in 2027, a local government, including voters using the initiative, could not impose, extend, or increase a special tax without a two-thirds vote, with stated exceptions. A higher bar for new local taxes matches this framework. The official framing, that the rule would bind voter initiatives and not only governing bodies, is the point the other way.

Legislative constitutional amendment. The qualified-list title says it limits voters’ ability to raise local revenue.

- [Numbering release](https://www.sos.ca.gov/administration/news-releases-and-advisories/2026-news-releases-and-advisories/secretary-state-shirley-n-weber-phd-assigns-numbers-november-ballot-measures-invites-ballot-arguments)
- [Qualified measures](https://www.sos.ca.gov/elections/ballot-measures/qualified-ballot-measures)

### Proposition 44 — Yes, Low

**Why yes.** It would require nonprofit federally qualified health centers to spend at least 90 percent of revenue on program services, with penalties the attorney general can define. The accountability direction matches the concentrated-benefit test. Whether 90 percent is a workable line was not established in the official summary. I am not confident here.

Initiative statute. San Ramon Patch describes the measure as union-backed and says penalties that are not refunded after five years can fund clinic-worker training, recruitment, and retention. The analyst’s estimate is state cost up to the low tens of millions a year, much of it covered by fees and penalties.

**What would settle it.** Whether 90 percent is a workable line, and how the penalty fund would work.

### Proposition 45 — Yes, Medium

**Why yes.** It would shorten environmental review and court challenges for specified housing, transportation, water, health, and clean-energy projects. Faster housing review matches the five-year and supply tests. Narrower court review is a rule-of-law point the other way.

Initiative statute. The analyst expects implementation costs in the tens of millions a year at first, likely net savings later, and an uncertain court effect.

- [Numbering release](https://www.sos.ca.gov/administration/news-releases-and-advisories/2026-news-releases-and-advisories/secretary-state-shirley-n-weber-phd-assigns-numbers-november-ballot-measures-invites-ballot-arguments)
- [Qualified measures](https://www.sos.ca.gov/elections/ballot-measures/qualified-ballot-measures)

### Measure RTM — No, Medium

**Why no.** It would add a retail sales tax of one-half of one percent in Contra Costa for 14 years, about $980 million a year, to avoid major transit cuts and to fund safety, repairs, and oversight. This framework disfavors new sales taxes, and the ballot question reads as revenue to cover an operating gap. Riders and the named repairs are a real benefit the other way, so this is Medium rather than High.

The county counsel’s analysis says the district was created by Senate Bill 63 (2025) and covers Alameda, Contra Costa, San Mateo, Santa Clara, and San Francisco. The San Francisco rate in that analysis is one percent. This pass did not review the yes and no campaign treasuries.
