# Worked example: November 3, 2026 general — San Ramon 94582

**Last verified: October 14, 2026.**

**Framework:** fairness-not-equity. Default weights 45 / 15 / 12 / 10 / 8 / 7 / 3. That order is policy alignment, five-year effects, concentrated benefit, individualism, delivery of framework-aligned results, feasibility, and competence. Feasibility is additive. It is not multiplied by policy.

**Voter:** the same San Ramon voter as `examples/2026-primary-ca10.md`. ZIP 94582, Contra Costa County, no party preference. Council district and school trustee area depend on the street address. This file does not assign them.

This is an analysis under one named framework. It is not a prediction and not an instruction to vote. Word and PDF copies are optional and were not produced.

Confidence labels follow `scoring/confidence-levels.md`. A row that was not scored does not get one.

## How to read the scores

Criterion scores are judgments tied to the sources under each race. A score near the middle on a criterion this pass did not research means "not researched," not "mediocre in fact."

Each composite is the weighted sum of seven separate scores. Policy alignment is not multiplied by feasibility. Delivery is scored on a framework-aligned result, not on having held the office. The arithmetic is under each race so it can be redone.

Readers who want other weights: replace the seven defaults with seven numbers that sum to 100, recompute every composite, and say the weights are yours. The method is in `scoring/weighted-criteria.md` and in `SKILL.md`.

## Statewide propositions

Each pick uses the measure tests in the framework, applied to the official summary. Measures are not scored on the seven candidate weights. High requires three tests the same way and no serious test the other way.

Summaries below are paraphrases of the June 29, 2026 numbering release unless another link is given. ([Numbering release](https://www.sos.ca.gov/administration/news-releases-and-advisories/2026-news-releases-and-advisories/secretary-state-shirley-n-weber-phd-assigns-numbers-november-ballot-measures-invites-ballot-arguments); [qualified list](https://www.sos.ca.gov/elections/ballot-measures/qualified-ballot-measures))

**Proposition 1. NO, Medium.** Legislative statute. Authorizes $11.25 billion in general-obligation bonds: $10 billion for affordable rental and homeownership programs, $1.25 billion for veterans' farm, home, and mobilehome purchase assistance. A new long-term debt repaid by taxpayers fails this framework's high bar for new obligations. The housing-supply purpose is a real point the other way, which is why this is not High.

**Proposition 2. YES, Medium.** Legislative constitutional amendment. The qualified-list title is an increase in the state's rainy-day fund. The amendment text in the numbering release raises the Budget Stabilization Account cap from 10 percent of general-fund tax proceeds to 20 percent, changes the capital-gains set-aside, and lets a May budget revision function as a budget-emergency proclamation when the constitutional conditions are met. The larger reserve matches the framework's fiscal-discipline test and is not a new tax. The easier path to declaring a budget emergency is a serious point the other way.

**Proposition 3. NO, Medium.** Initiative constitutional amendment. Makes the temporary high-income tax rates, now set to expire in 2031, permanent. The legislative analyst's summary in the release says that keeps $5 billion to $15 billion a year in today's dollars. Thresholds in the summary are about $360,000 for single filers, $721,000 for joint filers, and $490,000 for heads of household, in 2024 dollars. A permanent tax fails the high bar. The payers are high-income filers rather than a flat sales-tax base, and the stated split is 89 percent schools and 11 percent community colleges, so this is not High.

**Proposition 4. NO, Medium.** Legislative statute. Repeals the ban on spending public money on campaigns for elective office, except money earmarked for education, transportation, or public safety, and sets expenditure limits and qualifying rules. Taxpayers would fund campaigns, including candidates they oppose. That is a diffuse cost. The counter-argument, that it reduces donor dependence, is why this is Medium rather than High. This pass did not review the campaign's funders.

**Proposition 5. YES, Low.** Legislative constitutional amendment. The qualified-list title is a change to the recall process. The amendment eliminates the successor election when a statewide officer is recalled. The office is filled under the ordinary vacancy rules. If the governor is recalled, the lieutenant governor serves the rest of the term, with a special election in the first two years if the recall falls before the next nomination period closes. The recalled officer cannot be appointed to the vacancy. This is a process change. It does not clearly hit the tax test or the liberty test. The lean toward yes is that it drops a plurality side-election. Push back if you want the successor chosen on the same ballot. I am not confident here.

**Proposition 37. YES, Low.** Initiative statute. Authorizes up to $25 billion in bonds for fixed-rate loans of up to 17 percent of the price of a qualifying new home, for buyers under 200 percent of area median income who put at least 3 percent down. The summary says the bonds are repaid by the borrowers' payments, not by the state, and the analyst's estimate says no direct state or local cost. That fiscal note is why the framework does not treat this like Proposition 1. The structure is novel, so the confidence is Low. Push back if the repayment promise depends on a backstop this summary does not show.

**Proposition 38. NO, Medium.** Initiative statute. Authorizes $8.4 billion in general-obligation bonds for immunology research, half to a University of California-affiliated institute and half to grants, with half of the research money aimed at cancer, heart disease, and Alzheimer's. Recipients must sell resulting drugs in California at 20 percent below the national average price. The analyst's estimate is about $500 million a year for 25 years from the general fund. New long-term debt on diffuse taxpayers fails the high bar. The disease-research purpose and the price term are the point the other way.

**Proposition 39. YES, Medium.** Initiative constitutional amendment. Requires government-issued identification at the polls, or the last four digits of such an identification number on a mail ballot; requires the state to provide identification cards on request; requires counties to report each year the share of voters whose citizenship they have verified. The analyst's estimate is tens of millions of dollars one time, and ongoing costs from the tens of millions to the low hundreds of millions. One identification rule for every voter matches this framework's rule-equality test. The cost, and the risk that a lawful voter is turned away, are the point the other way. This pass did not review evidence about how often that happens.

**Proposition 40. NO, Medium.** Initiative constitutional amendment and statute. A one-time tax of up to 5 percent on covered assets above $1 billion, excluding real property and some retirement accounts. Ninety percent of the revenue is for health care and 10 percent for food assistance or education. The revenue is exempt from school-funding formulas, budget-reserve rules, and the state spending limit. The analyst's estimate says the tax would raise tens of billions over several years and that income-tax revenue would likely fall by hundreds of millions of dollars or more per year afterward. A new tax plus a spending-limit opt-out fails the fiscal tests. It is not High, because the payers are a narrow class and the tax is one time, not permanent.

**Proposition 41. YES, High.** Initiative constitutional amendment. For statewide special taxes, requires a pre-election audit of the programs an initiative would fund, and recurring audits of programs funded by special taxes enacted after January 1, 2026. Prohibits new state taxes, enacted after that date, that exclude their revenue from the existing spending limit, including taxes on the same ballot. Three tests point the same way: it does not create a tax, it blocks spending-limit opt-outs, and it requires audits of the programs those taxes would fund. The analyst says the net fiscal effect is unknown. That is uncertainty about magnitude, not a test pointing the other way.

**Proposition 42. YES, High.** Initiative constitutional amendment. Prohibits a new state tax on the ownership of personal property, including retirement accounts, financial assets, business interests, and intellectual property, and prohibits certain retroactive state taxes. It applies to taxes enacted or taking effect on or after January 1, 2026, including taxes on the same ballot. The analyst's estimate is that future tax revenue might be lower. Under this framework that lower revenue is the intended effect, not a conflicting test. The three aligned tests are: no new tax of this kind, no retroactive tax of the kind described, and the rule covers same-ballot measures.

**Proposition 43. YES, Medium.** Legislative constitutional amendment. The qualified-list title says it limits voters' ability to raise local revenue. Beginning January 1, 2027, a local government, including voters using the initiative, could not impose, extend, or increase a special tax without a two-thirds vote, with stated exceptions. A higher bar for local special taxes matches the framework's skepticism of new taxes. The official framing, that the same rule would bind voter initiatives and not only governing bodies, is the point the other way.

**Proposition 44. YES, Low.** Initiative statute. Requires nonprofit federally qualified health centers to spend at least 90 percent of revenue on program services. The attorney general may define qualifying spending. Shortfalls can be penalized, with a refund if the center complies within five years. False reports can be charged as crimes. The analyst's estimate is state cost up to the low tens of millions a year, much of it covered by fees and penalties. The accountability direction matches the concentrated-benefit test. Whether 90 percent is a workable line was not established in the summary. Push back. I am not confident here.

**Proposition 45. YES, Medium.** Initiative statute. Amends the California Environmental Quality Act to speed review of specified housing, transportation, water, health, and clean-energy projects: deadlines for the agency, a narrower alternatives analysis, and shorter, narrower court review. The analyst expects implementation costs in the tens of millions a year at first, and likely net savings later, with an uncertain court effect. Faster housing review matches this framework's five-year and housing-supply tests. Narrower court review is a rule-of-law point the other way.
